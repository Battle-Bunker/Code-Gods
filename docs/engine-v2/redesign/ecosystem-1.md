# What keeps a system alive, and what this game has of it (thinker: ecosystem-1, 16 November 2026)

Nothing here is built or simulated. Labels: **[C]** true by construction (follows from the rules as written);
**[O]** observed somewhere, with where and how narrowly; **[J]** my conjecture. The five analyses are about height,
price and score. This one is about what the players are to each other.

## The one idea

The game has a carefully made *landscape* and almost no *ecology*. Players differ only in what they **want** (the dealt
rule). They do not differ in what they **can do**, in **where they are**, or in what they **have built** [C]. In every
lasting system I can name, those three differences are where the life is: what one party makes or stores becomes food,
shelter, tool or obstacle for another. Here the only thing a player makes that others meet is a handful of numbers on
an object, and the next move overwrites it [C].

A test for "faked" dynamism: switch off the dealer (no rule redraw, no births, no retirements) and ask what still
moves. Scripted boards stood still in about two minutes [O: a dozen games a row, six one-step strategies, no talk; it
may be the scripts, not the game]. Movement that needs the dealer is weather, not ecology [J].

## 1. Six ingredients

1. **What is made stays, and becomes a part or a place for others.** Clearest in technology and open source; in nature,
   soil and beaver dams. Each new thing enlarges the set of possible next things, so the search never ends [O:
   the history of technology; not a law]. Artificial life shows the reverse: Tierra and Avida produced parasites and
   then stalled [O: those two systems]; the usual reading is that their worlds never grew [J].
2. **Abilities differ, under a budget that binds.** Clearest in card-game decks and animal bodies. Nobody can do
   everything, so every design has a counter and its worth depends on what is common (frequency dependence). Rock,
   paper, scissors is the thinnest case: three fixed moves, and "play each a third of the time" is an answer a
   solver finds [C]. The cycle lasts in living metagames because the list is open: a fourth thing can be invented [J].
3. **Stored value can be taken, not only destroyed.** Clearest in predator and prey. Whatever accumulates becomes
   someone's food, so accumulation creates its own opponent, and the eaters are limited by what there is to eat [C in
   the textbook model; O: lynx and hare records, loosely].
4. **Locality.** Clearest in a laboratory rock-paper-scissors among three bacterial strains: all three lasted on a
   plate, where each meets only neighbours, and one took over in a stirred flask [O: one experiment, Kerr and
   colleagues, 2002]. Distance stops one outcome spreading everywhere and keeps variety in store.
5. **What matters is private, costly to learn, and changed by acting on it.** Clearest in poker and markets. Using
   what you know reveals it, and the others then adapt, so what is hidden is an intention, never a fixed fact [J].
6. **More than one clock.** Clearest in language and science: fast use on slow structures (conventions, reputations,
   standards) that use itself slowly shifts. This gives regimes, and regime changes that somebody must notice [J].

## 2. Which of these the game has

| Ingredient | Verdict | The mechanism or the absence |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Made things persist as parts | **Lacks** | Objects do not combine. An agent program is private and cannot be called by another. The nearest thing is a lock (a player-written test, with energy on it, that others can pay into), and locks are not in the live game. The score product makes my lift your gain, but through one number. All [C]. |
| 2. Differing abilities | **Lacks** | Every seat has every mutator and every lens, and is offered every object. A do-everything program costs about 45 energy of about 2,530, so being a generalist is free [C]. Nothing forces a matchup. |
| 3. Takeable value | **Lacks** | Lift is paid and gone; coming down returns nothing to anyone [C]. So there is vandalism but no predation. An outsider knocks for spite or rank only. |
| 4. Locality | **Lacks** | A stirred flask: offers go to any agent on any object, in proportion to energy held [C]. No object is near another. |
| 5. Private, shifting information | **Thin, then faked** | Real: secret rules, paid reads, a noisy sum of the others' bits. But the hidden thing is a fixed card. Once inferred it is an exam about a rival, and the redraw every 120 seconds renews it from outside [C]. |
| 6. Several clocks | **Faked** | One ten-minute clock; the score is the last instant; I found nothing a game leaves behind for the next. The slow changes (redraw, turnover) are scheduled by the dealer, not caused by play [C, for what I read]. |

Two smaller notes. *Neutral drift* (moving freely among equally good states until a door opens) is truly present:
rules that read different numbers let me move along your level set. It shows up as the "sideways knock" because the
mover cannot see whose value they cross [C]. *Increasing returns* are present twice with no counterweight: the product
pays for piling onto one object, and offers follow energy held [C].

The owner's thesis names what people are good at: reading intent, making and breaking alliances, noticing a changed
regime, acting when it is unclear. Each needs an ingredient. Intent needs rivals with real choices (2, 3). Alliances
need something to exchange and something to betray (1, 2, 3). Regimes need slow structure (6). Today a person's main
edge is writing a better search program, and that is the solver's job [J].

## 3. Three changes of structure

**A. Stored energy can be taken.** When a player moves an object down, a share of the height released goes to that
player.

- *What it makes possible.* The owner wrote that hoarding wealth "depends on investment in defence", and that tall
  objects should be harder to defend. Both presuppose theft; the machine has only vandalism. With capture, the
  reward for attacking grows with height, and the lock that deters it must be about as large as the share an attacker
  would take. Both wants then follow [C] and need no separate rule. A lock becomes a shell. Raiding pays less the more
  raiders there are, because unguarded height is finite [C for the energy reward only; the rank reward is untouched].
  Born objects that happen to be tall become food to forage [C].
- *Cost.* A channel for one player to feed another (I lift, you eat). Possibly smaller income, since some would now be
  foraged. Up and down prices still differ by a fixed multiple of the height change, so the bookkeeping holds [C].
- *How it could be wrong.* Everyone raids, nobody builds, and the board is a flat desert. In nature, prey survive
  through refuges and distance; here that is locks and change C, both untested. A person may find the eat-or-build
  sum easy.

**B. Seats differ in what they can do.** Each seat holds a small toolkit of mutators, dealt or chosen under a budget.
Height stays one shared thing; which routes exist, and what they cost in friction, differ by seat.

- *What it makes possible.* A state that is far for me is near for you [C]. That is comparative advantage, the root of
  trade, and it gives alliances content: I need your stretch, you need my swap, and either can withhold. It is also
  the owner's "each mutator its own cost landscape", obtained without a second energy function [C]. Inference gets a
  second subject: not only what a rival wants but what they can do.
- *Cost.* Balance; more for a 12-year-old to hold; and it needs a means of payment, which the owner's lock nearly is.
  A lock makes leaving a set of states cost energy. The same stake with the sign turned pays whoever brings the object
  in. One primitive, "energy staked on a test", would cover defence and hiring.
- *How it could be wrong.* It becomes luck of the deal ("whoever got stretch wins"). Players may ignore one another and
  do what they can alone. Chat may dissolve it into "tell me what to move".

**C. Objects and agents have places.** Objects sit on a ring or a small map; an agent is offered only what is near it,
and moving costs energy.

- *What it makes possible.* Different corners can be in different states (raided, walled, pioneer), with fronts moving
  between them. A refuge exists, which A needs. Where to be is a decision, and so is who my neighbours are.
- *Cost.* It splits a small table: with three seats and five objects there may be too little to be local about. It
  needs more objects, or objects that drift past.
- *How it could be wrong.* The one experiment I cite is bacteria, not people who can talk across the map. Locality
  could simply make three private gardens, which the product then pays nothing for.

If only one: A. It is the smallest change, and it turns two of the owner's stated wants from added rules into
consequences.

## 4. Two unusual ideas

**Rules that read the other objects.** Every dealt rule today scores an object against fixed mathematics (a prime, a
distance, a pattern). Let some rules be relative: "my line is steeper than every other line on the board", "this grid
has the largest corner total", "parallel to whichever line moved last". Then a target moves whenever a rival moves, and
no board satisfies two opposed relative rules for good [C]. This is why trees are tall: height is worth nothing alone
and everything against neighbours. It answers the owner's "unbounded hills, with adversaries" with adversaries who are
players, not other cards. It also ties objects together, a kind of locality that comes from the rules. *How it could be
wrong:* a bare race is a dull auction won by the bigger spender; it needs gravity to bind and the other rules to cut
across it. It is a change to dealt rules, so it is the coordinator's to trial and declare, and must pass the design
principles.

**The ecosystem may live between games, not inside one.** In card games, fighting games and chess openings, the single
match resolves; what never settles is the population of decks or styles across thousands of matches [O: those games,
broadly]. Here the lasting artefact is the agent program, and the dealt rule is the weather it must survive. Let
programs persist, be published, forked, and called by other programs for a small royalty to the author. Then players
do build parts for others (ingredient 1) and there is a slow clock (ingredient 6). *How it could be wrong:* without
changes A or B there may be one best program, which everybody copies, and that is a monoculture. And it may pull human
attention off the board and into code, which may not be the game the owner wants.

## 5. What I could not see

- People playing. Everything "observed" about this game is scripts. Chat may already carry alliances I call absent.
- Whether anything persists between games (ratings, saved programs, rematches). I found no sign, in a short read.
- What v1 did about any of this. I did not read the archive.
- Whether capture (A) breaks the rarity tables. If score stays "rarity under a random walk", a walk that is paid to
  descend is a different walk.
- Sizes. Three seats and five objects may be too small a world for any ecology, whatever the rules; I have no way to
  know the threshold from here.
- My own bias. Each proposal is a mechanism, and I was asked for structure. The honest version of this document may be
  only sections 1 and 2: the game's differences between players are all of one kind.
