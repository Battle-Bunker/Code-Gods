THE GAME, in one paragraph. Several players (people with AI assistants, or AI seats) share a board of mathematical objects (lines y = mx + c with rational coefficients; 4×4 grids of digits; pairs of lines). Each player is secretly dealt a "rule": a preference over object states (a utility function, drawn from parameterised families, with layered secondary preferences). Players do not move objects by hand; they write small programs ("agents") that are offered objects over time and may mutate them, paying energy. Energy arrives as income over a ten-minute game. A player's points on an object = their own "bits" on it × the sum of the other players' bits on it, so objects that are good for several players at once pay most. Standing is by total points.

WHAT THE OWNER HAS SAID THEY WANT (verbatim where quoted; these are the properties to produce, by whatever simplest machine):
1. "We're trying to design a metastable ecosystem here that remains dynamic and interesting and we're explicitly trying to move away from a task that's like a static exam." The landscape over the players' combined utility functions should be enormous and hard to search "even for a frontier AI with a substantial amount of compute". "If you discover that optimal *solutions* are ever being found you should report this as a bug."
2. "All strategies should be incremental hill climbing towards profitable combined states." Agents "accept temporary losses to rise to higher potential scoring places in the combined utility landscape between cooperating players".
3. "We want a meaningful progression of expensively obtained bigger solutions to a preference that are rewarded commensurate with the expense of reaching them."
4. "Our means of measuring the energy/bit value of a state is supposed to be directly connected to how much energy it takes to worth of mutations to reach that state." "My intent is that we design the costing system so that it's structurally hard for any rule to enable significantly more score bits in a game than par rather than needing to give dedicated attention per rule." Energy is measured in bits.
5. Gravity: "We want gravity towards smaller numbers to be a configurable force size ... scaled up considerably so that it really behaves like a force of gravity. Think about appropriate physics metaphors which work naturally with the scoring math we already have and ideally have deep alignment with the information theory lens. We want strong gravity so that it becomes increasingly rare and energy expensive to reach bigger solutions and they score commensurately more in energy/bits because it would be rarer for random mutations to reach them". If one global energy function is needed, "it should charge smoothly with the actual size of the number, perhaps with a non-linearity inspired by actual gravity".
6. Exact hits: "if it is in fact easy/cheap to get from near to an integer to exactly the integer ... the difference in energy score between states near to the integer and exactly on it should be modest."
7. Robustness: "Rules should still keep some of their value when modest sized perturbations occur." They were angry at "absurdly fragile" rules where players "climb up a tenuous ladder of points and then [have] the rug pulled back to zero".
8. Repair against damage: "Repair should always be more expensive than damage. We designed our scoring system specifically so that it's more expensive to push states to higher energy than lower energy states." And: "it's good that it becomes increasingly hard to defend higher energy/scoring objects because of the asymmetry in cheaply imposing costs relative to repairing what was destroyed growing in severity as energy rises. That mirrors real life ecosystem dynamics ... But I also want it to be more possible to defend higher scores with extra investment of energy. In real life, the accumulation of concentrations of energy and wealth by intelligent agents depends on investment in defence."
9. Locks (their proposed defence mechanic): a lock on an object with a predicate the object currently meets; breaking the predicate requires paying off the lock; "Predicates should just be programs with the same degrees of freedom as agents and the same complexity scoring rules in terms of information read and program description length"; upkeep proportional to predicate complexity; anyone offered the object may add or subtract energy. "The kind of metagame I am envisioning involves coops of players designing integrated strategies ... It's ok for specific states to be calculated in advance and then effortfully pursued by multiple cooperating agents over many steps (and then locked collaboratively too), as long as such high value states can't be easily reached in one step and as long as multiple distinct high value states need to be found to do well in aggregate."
10. Diversity, not inversion: "a diversity of rules that are highly perpendicular on average such that there will reliably be rules that pull against each other". Unbounded "bigger is better" hills are wanted; no maxima. Rules are parameterised families with a real parameter space, and variable-length preference tuples: "primary preferences that use nearest desired value and then a secondary preference that scores proximity to it and then ... a tertiary preference to integrate another dimension". Rules live on rationals, not just integers. A rule card must let a 12-year-old infer the scoring.
11. Shortcuts: mutators that work in another representation (swap, rotate, mirror, stretch by a factor) are wanted as differently-priced routes: "That makes the state space more interestingly shaped in terms of cost function topology and more like real life exploration of problem spaces."
12. Mixed costing as the owner understood it: adding costs in proportion to the amount; multiplying costs by digits; "the mixed strategy elegantly still makes high primes hard to reach because multiplying a number doesn't reach a prime so the more expensive addition mutator is needed". They assumed each mutator could have its own cost landscape; they were told one global energy function is required and were unhappy that this was patched rather than explained.

THE MACHINE AS IT STANDS (verify everything in the code; this summary may be wrong in places, and finding where is part of the job):
- State energy φ(s): a code length in bits for the object's numbers. Today for lines (primitive integer triples a, b, c of ax + by + c = 0) it is a FACTORISATION code for a and c (half a bit per prime factor with multiplicity: 64 → 6 bits, 37 → 18.5, 101 → 50.5) and half a bit per unit for b. For the 4×4 digit grid φ is half a bit per unit of the cell sum. The owner has rejected the factorisation code.
- The "law": the stationary distribution π(s) ∝ 2^−φ(s) of a random walk over the mutators; it is what rarity is measured against.
- Prices: a move costs a fee (the code length of the call: additive amounts linear in size, factors by digits; a move and its inverse share the fee) plus a surcharge max(0, Δφ). Prices are an "exact potential" (detailed balance), which is what makes π known in closed form. An act (several calls) costs W + βW².
- Score bits of a state for a rule = −log2 of the law's probability of scoring at least this well ("rarity"). So bits(s) ≤ φ(s) roughly, and bits gained ≤ energy paid in surcharge. With layered rules the valuation became a "tree": level bits + closeness bits + third bits, each a conditional rarity; inner parts were just capped at 2 bits each as a patch.
- Income is about 3,000 energy per seat per game; a "par" game is about 25 bits. So one energy buys one bit of φ, and seats have about 100× more energy than the bit scale. A gate test forbids any rule passing 35 bits on a whole income.
- Measured (simulation, see docs): boards reach standstill in about two minutes; late play is two-state flip-flopping; greedy starters stop at the first exact hit; a strategy "build, and knock a rival when that pays more per energy" beats naive starters (58 to 67% wins) and on lines is the best reply to itself (everyone loses 36% of points); 94% of knocks on grids and a third on lines are "sideways" in φ (swaps, mirrors, neighbouring integers): they destroy score at no change in energy, so the undo costs the same; on grids no high-scoring board is higher in φ than its neighbours, because rules reward patterns that the cell sum cannot see; on lines a high score is a high-φ state about half the time. A size-based φ (constant field, g bits per unit) makes levels carry the bits and exact hits modest, but at one energy per bit climbers reach 45 to 340 bits; at 100 energy per bit, 8 to 44.
- Patches added over time that you should treat as symptoms: the 35-bit gate; per-rule exemptions in the rule check; the 2-bit cap on inner tree parts; floors for undefined readings; a proposed convex surcharge (c′); "energy weight" of 100 per bit; hold rarity (rejected); tie-breaker tiers; the factorisation code itself; two-step lookahead starters; turnover (objects retire and new ones are born) to stop flip-flopping.
